However, deploying these models in real-world scenarios, particularly on resource-constrained devices, remains challenging due to their substantial computational demands. This has spurred interest in distilling knowledge from large VLMs into smaller, more efficient counterparts. A key challenge arises here from the diversity of VLM architectures, which are built on different LLMs and employ varying token types—differing in vocabulary size, token splits, and token index ordering. To address this challenge of limitation to a specific VLM type, we present Gen eration after Recal ibration (GenRecal), a novel, general-purpose distillation framework for VLMs. GenRecal incorporates a Recalibrator that aligns and adapts feature representations between heterogeneous VLMs, enabling effective knowledge transfer across different types of VLMs. Through extensive experiments on multiple challenging benchmarks, we demonstrate that GenRecal significantly improves baseline performances, eventually outperforming large-scale open- and closed-source VLMs. [[Project Page]](https://byungkwanlee.github.io/GenRecal-page)\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\n0 0 footnotetext: ††\\dagger† Work Done during Internship.![Image 3: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1/x1.png)\n\nFigure 1: (Left) Visualizing the token indices of a given image and text prompt and representing the possibility of distillation among various VLM pair combinations, comparing traditional distillation with our proposed distillation framework, GenRecal. Recent advancements in VLMs[[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib4), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib5), [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib6)] have leveraged large-scale language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning and generation capabilities by incorporating extensive textual knowledge[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib7), [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib8)]. In pursuit of superior performance, state-of-the-art VLMs[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib9), [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib10), [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib11), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib12), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib13)] now integrate scaled-up LLMs such as 72B model parameters, achieving results comparable to proprietary models like GPT-4V[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib14)] and Claude-3.5 Sonnet[[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib15)].\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nDespite these advances, the growing model sizes introduce substantial computational burdens, limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios, particularly for on-device deployment. To address this challenge, distillation techniques have been explored to transfer knowledge from large VLMs to smaller, more efficient models. This process typically follows two approaches: (a) text-based distillation using crafted text answer responses and (b) feature-based distillation via layer supervision. The former has been adopted in standard VLM training by leveraging high-quality visual instruction datasets[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib9), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib16)], often curated using high-performing proprietary models like GPT-4V[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib14)]. In contrast, the latter requires access to internal model features, which limits its applicability to open-source VLMs. In detail, recent studies[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib17), [18](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib18), [19](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib19)] have focused on directly distilling intermediate layers’ features or logits on language heads (i.e., last layer’s outputs).\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nHowever, existing distillation methods that attempt to transfer knowledge beyond the natural language space—such as logits from the language head or token spaces—are largely limited by a fundamental constraint: they only work when the large (teacher) and small (student) VLMs share the same vocabulary sizes, token splits, and token index ordering schemes (i.e., what we refer to as token types), as shown on the left side of Figure[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models"). Note that different image token splitting strategies for each VLM can also cause distillation to fail, as discussed in Appendix[A](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#A1 "Appendix A Token Type Examples ‣ 4.3 Analysis of Recalibrator ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models"). For example, the InternVL2.5 series[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib13)] splits an image into up to 12 segments based on its actual size, whereas the Qwen2-VL series[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib10)] does not split the image—instead, it employs a multimodal RoPE technique to dynamically handle a single-shot image. These differences in token types among various VLMs naturally result in significantly different total input or output token lengths. Consequently, this restriction limits the opportunities to distill knowledge from more powerful VLMs that have different token types from those of the student VLMs. Various combination lists are more described in Appendix[B](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#A2 "Appendix B Possible Distillation Pair for VLM Combinations: Traditional Distillation versus GenRecal ‣ Appendix A Token Type Examples ‣ 4.3 Analysis of Recalibrator ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models").\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nTo overcome this limitation, we propose Gen eration after Recal ibration (GenRecal), a novel framework for token types-compatible VLM distillation. At its core, GenRecal introduces a Recalibrator that aligns and adapts the feature representations of small VLMs with those of large VLMs. Recalibrator is trained to bridge the gap between the large and small VLMs by projecting small VLM features into one representation space compatible with the teacher VLMs’ features. In the end, this mechanism brings in general-purpose distillation based on the shared feature representation through Recalibrator. To prove the necessity of GenRecal, we first compare the performances of traditional distillation and GenRecal, as displayed in experiment types C and D at Figure[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models"). Although we employ same token types of VLMs (Qwen2-VL-72B and Qwen2-VL7B), GenRecal outperforms traditional distillation implemented by LLaVA-KD[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib17)]. This suggests that matching feature representations between large and small VLMs is really crucial point to seamlessly conduct distillation with less information loss.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\n![Image 4: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1/x2.png)\n\nFigure 2: (Left) Comparison of the challenging benchmark performances, MMB[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib20)], MM-Vet[[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib1)], MMMU[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib21)], and MMMU-Pro[[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib22)] by changing teacher vision-language models (VLMs) to distill the knowledge into small VLMs. Notably, the more powerful the teacher VLMs we select, the greater the performance improvement we can achieve. Hence, it is necessary to develop small VLMs for embedded on the light devices and fast inference, and this demand has led to the emergence of two types of distillation approaches. First one is constructing visual instruction tuning datasets[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib9), [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib16), [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib23), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib24), [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib25), [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib26), [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib27), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib28)] from large-scale VLMs and training small VLMs with those curated datasets. This dataset includes image’s question-answer pairs generated from GPT-4V or human review. Second one is conducting distillation of feature representation. LLaVA-MoD[[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib18)] conducts logits-based distillation on mixture of expert (MoE)[[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib29), [30](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib30)] architectures, while using DPO[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib31)] with positive or negative preference responses. LLaVA-KD[[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib17)] also uses logits-based distillation but presents three-stage training process where trainable parameters are different for each stage to effectively warm-up and distill the knowledge. Align-KD[[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib19)] distills both vision encoder features and decoder logits, while encouraging the student VLMs’ first decoder layer’s attention map to resemble that of the large VLM. MoVE-KD[[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib32)] employs multiple vision encoders to large VLMs and utilize mixture of LoRA[[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib33)] to small VLM. It distills visual information of large VLMs by using visual attention map. Besides, there are earlier distillation studies (see Appendix[C](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#A3 "Appendix C Extended Related Works ‣ Appendix B Possible Distillation Pair for VLM Combinations: Traditional Distillation versus GenRecal ‣ Appendix A Token Type Examples ‣ 4.3 Analysis of Recalibrator ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models")), but they do not consider either discrete token-derived or different token type distillation.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\n![Image 5: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1/x3.png)\n\nFigure 3: Overview of GenRecal architecture and its training stages. For large VLMs, we choose model sizes over 72B to have competitive performances: NVLM-72B (Qwen2-72B)[[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib12)], Qwen2-VL-72B (Qwen2-72B)[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib10)], and InternVL2-76B (Llama3-70B)[[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib34)], and InternVL2.5-78B (Qwen2.5-72B)[[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib13)]. Next, we also select various model sizes of small VLMs: Qwen2-VL-2B/7B (Qwen2-2B/7B) and InternVL2.5-1B/2B/4B/8B (InternLM2.5-1.8B/7B and Qwen2.5-0.5B/3B/72B). Note that, the parentheses mean LLM used in their VLMs. Starting from this section, we refer to four segment modules in a VLM architecure as vision encoder, vision projector, VLM-body, and VLM-head (i.e., language head). We build Recalibrator that plays a role in acquiring shared features on one representation space. Recalibrator.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nAs described in Section[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#S3 "3 GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models"), Recalibrator is composed of two transformer decoder blocks (Rec-body) and two projectors (Rec-proj-pre and Rec-proj-post). The decoder blocks follow all configurations of small VLMs’ decoder block such as causal mask, hidden dimensions, number of heads, and FFN structure. In addition, the projectors include the one-layer linear module. For propagation in Recalibrator, there are some critical techniques that should be considered. New positional embedding should be assigned to input sequence for Recalib-body. This is because the input sequence is based on question features in small VLMs and answer features in large VLMs, so they naturally have different type of positional embeddings and wrongly matched position-ids as well, when we are concatenating the features from large and small VLMs. Therefore, we employ another RoPE[[48](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib48)] to realign their positional embeddings, and their position-ids are re-assigned as well. Lastly, we apply additional layer-norm[[49](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib49)] into the output features of Recalibrator for stable adaptation, before meeting VLM-head of large VLM.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\n#### (b) Visual Instruction Tuning Dataset.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nWe gather 9M visual instruction tuning dataset covering a wide range of vision-language capabilities such as general visual question answering, dense image captioning, chart/diagram/document understanding, common-sense knowledge, science and math understanding, and multi-dimensional reasoning. It suggests that even though large and small VLMs are sharing same token types, their feature alignment is required to reduce their feature disparity. Moreover, when building GenRecal, VLM-head of large VLMs is ‘only’ used to make distillation into small VLMs (see Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Related Works ‣ GenRecal: Generation after Recalibration from Large to Small Vision-Language Models")(a) and (b)), while traditional distillation uses VLM-head of small VLMs to inject the knowledge into small VLMs. It indicates that GenRecal can receive more rich knowledge than traditional distillation, because VLM-head of large VLMs fundamentally has more larger hidden dimension than that of small VLMs.\n\nReport issue for preceding element\n\nWe present a new, efficient family of vision-language models (VLMs) called Gen eration after Recal ibration (GenRecal). Initially, research concentrated on aligning the output logits of a “teacher” model with those produced by its “student”[[90](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib90), [91](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib91)]. Not long after, the FitNet framework[[92](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib92)] expanded this notion by guiding the student with intermediate feature representations instead of solely relying on final outputs. In this configuration, convolutional layers transform the student’s features to mirror those of the teacher, with any divergence measured by an L2 loss. Spurred by this single-stage, feature-based approach, subsequent techniques delved deeper into leveraging internal representations for effective knowledge transfer. For instance, investigations by [[93](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib93), [94](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib94), [95](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib95), [96](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib96)] revealed that incorporating multiple internal layers from the teacher significantly enhances the distillation process. One noteworthy method, PKT[[97](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib97)], recast the teacher’s knowledge as a probability distribution and employed KL divergence to align it with the student’s outputs. In a similar fashion, RKD[[98](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib98)] concentrated on capturing the relationships between samples, while CRD[[99](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib99)] combined contrastive learning objectives with traditional distillation techniques. Moreover, multi-stage and relational strategies soon emerged. For example, AT[[100](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib100)] made use of attention maps extracted from several teacher layers, and FSP[[101](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib101)] utilized matrices derived from feature maps to guide the student. Further refinements followed: SP[[102](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib102)] assessed the similarity among input samples rather than individual features, and OFD[[103](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib103)] introduced a novel distance metric based on marginal ReLU activations to capture essential information. As the methodology evolved, two principal strategies became apparent. On-policy techniques[[104](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib104), [67](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib67), [105](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib105), [66](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib66)] dynamically sample data during training, whereas off-policy methods[[67](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib67), [93](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib93), [91](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib91), [106](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib106), [107](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib107)] rely on pre-existing datasets. While initial efforts primarily involved single-teacher frameworks[[105](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib105), [66](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib66), [18](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib18), [108](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib108)], integrating multiple teachers has gradually attracted attention despite challenges such as architectural differences, vocabulary mismatches, and task misalignment[[109](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib109), [110](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib110), [111](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib111)]. Indeed, approaches like DKMF[[112](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib112)] and FNKD[[113](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib113)] have demonstrated the benefits of harnessing teacher feature representations, with DGKD[[114](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib114)] further consolidating knowledge from various teachers to boost student performance. More recent endeavors have concentrated on refining the loss functions employed during distillation. MiniLLM[[66](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib66)] and DistiLLM[[67](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib67)] have proposed adaptations of KL divergence—such as reverse or skewed variants—to mitigate overfitting, particularly in cases involving long-tail predictions. Additionally, [[115](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15681v1#bib.bib115)] put forward a dynamic loss-balancing strategy that adaptively fuses conventional and reverse KL divergence during training. Finally, to enhance the reasoning capabilities of the distilled models, contemporary research has incorporated chain-of-thought techniques.
